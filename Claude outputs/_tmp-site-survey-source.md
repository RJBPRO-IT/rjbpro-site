# What a Site Survey Should Catch Before You Bid

A drawing may show where a new wall belongs. It may not show the damaged flooring beneath an existing fixture, the equipment blocking the delivery route, or the utility connection that moved during a previous renovation.

Those details can affect the cost and sequence of a commercial construction project. Discovering them after work begins leaves less time to evaluate options and coordinate a solution.

A pre-bid site survey helps connect the proposed scope with the building as it exists today. For retailers, property owners, and construction teams, that means better information before pricing decisions are made.

Here is what a useful survey should capture.

# 1. Where Existing Conditions Differ From the Plans

Existing drawings are a starting point. Renovations, repairs, equipment replacements, and tenant changes can leave a building with conditions that differ from the available documents.

A survey should compare the proposed work with visible field conditions, including:

• Room dimensions and layout.

• Ceiling heights and accessible overhead spaces.

• Door openings and storefront configurations.

• Columns and other visible structural elements.

• Fixture and equipment locations.

• Floor elevations and transitions between spaces.

If a proposed installation does not fit the available space, the project team needs to know before the estimate assumes it will.

Documenting a discrepancy does not resolve the design. It gives the estimator and design team a clear question to address.

# 2. The Condition of Surfaces Being Removed, Reused, or Repaired

A finish replacement often involves more than the new material.

Removing fixtures may expose unfinished flooring. Taking down signage may leave damaged walls. Replacing a ceiling may reveal previous repairs or visible conflicts that require further investigation.

The survey should document:

• Visible damage to floors, walls, and ceilings.

• Flooring transitions and exposed substrate conditions.

• Areas requiring patching after demolition.

• Finishes that must match adjacent surfaces.

• Items designated to remain and requiring protection.

• Visible staining, deterioration, or signs of moisture that warrant follow-up.

For example, relocating a checkout may leave floor penetrations and gaps in the existing finish. Identifying those conditions helps the team clarify whether the scope includes localized repairs or a larger flooring replacement.

# 3. Utility Locations and Questions That Need Trade Review

A new fixture or piece of equipment may require power, data, plumbing, ventilation, or connections to other building systems.

A survey should record visible equipment, connection points, labels, and routing where safely accessible. It should also identify questions for the appropriate trade or design professional, such as:

• Is the proposed connection location consistent with the plans?

• Is existing equipment being reused, relocated, or replaced?

• Will the work require a shutdown?

• Are utility routes accessible?

• Is additional testing or capacity verification needed?

• Who is responsible for disconnecting and reconnecting equipment?

A visual survey cannot establish every system's condition or available capacity. Its value includes identifying what remains unverified so the estimate does not quietly depend on an unsupported assumption.

# 4. How Crews and Materials Will Reach the Work Area

A project can have a straightforward scope and difficult logistics.

Before pricing, the team should understand how workers, tools, materials, and debris will move through the property.

Useful survey information includes:

• Loading areas and delivery entrances.

• Door dimensions along the delivery route.

• Stair or elevator access.

• Available staging and storage space.

• Potential dumpster or container locations.

• Parking and equipment access.

• Shared corridors or areas controlled by the landlord.

• Restrictions on deliveries and debris removal.

If materials must be carried through a long occupied corridor, labor planning may differ from a location with direct access to the work area. If storage is limited, the team may need smaller, coordinated deliveries.

Those details belong in the estimate and execution plan.

# 5. The Operating Conditions Around the Construction

For an occupied store, the survey should include a conversation with the people who operate the location.

Store teams can help identify delivery schedules, high-traffic periods, employee access needs, and areas that cannot be taken out of service without coordination.

Questions should include:

• What work hours are permitted?

• Which areas must remain accessible?

• When can noisy or disruptive work occur?

• How will construction affect receiving and stocking?

• Are temporary barriers or directional signs needed?

• What daily cleanup and reopening expectations apply?

• Who approves shutdowns or temporary closures?

A survey should record these requirements and flag anything that still needs owner approval. Overnight work, temporary protection, and daily setup and breakdown can all influence pricing.

# 6. Scope Boundaries and Responsibilities

Some of the most useful survey findings are questions about who owns a task.

A piece of equipment may be owner-furnished, but responsibility for receiving, unloading, storage, installation, and final connections still needs to be established.

The same applies to fixtures, signage, security equipment, and vendor-managed systems.

Before bidding, clarify:

• What the contractor supplies.

• What the owner or landlord supplies.

• What separate vendors install.

• Who coordinates deliveries and access.

• Who removes or disposes of existing items.

• What repair work follows another vendor's installation.

• Where one trade's scope ends and another begins.

A site survey can reveal these coordination needs, but they should be resolved through the project's formal clarification process so bidders price consistent expectations.

# 7. Areas That Could Not Be Verified

A useful survey report should clearly identify its limits.

A locked mechanical room, inaccessible roof, concealed utility route, or occupied storage area may prevent the team from verifying a condition. That limitation should appear in the report alongside the items successfully documented.

Each unresolved item should indicate:

• What could not be checked.

• Why access or verification was limited.

• How it could affect the proposed work.

• What follow-up is needed.

Further investigation may require another visit, specialist review, testing, or owner-authorized exploratory work. A visual walkthrough should not be treated as confirmation of concealed conditions.

# Turn Survey Findings Into Bid Decisions

Photos and measurements only help if the estimating team can use them.

A strong survey package connects each significant finding to a location, a scope item, and a next step. Labeled photographs, marked-up plans, measurements, and a concise list of open questions make the information easier to review.

Before submitting a bid, the team should determine which findings require:

• An RFI or design clarification.

• Additional trade pricing.

• A revised quantity or scope.

• A logistics or phasing adjustment.

• An explicit qualification or allowance, where permitted by the bid requirements.

A pre-bid site survey cannot uncover every concealed condition. It can help the project team separate what is known from what still needs an answer.
