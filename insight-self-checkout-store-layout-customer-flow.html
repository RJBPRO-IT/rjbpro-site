Self-Checkout and Store Layouts: Building a Better Customer Flow

September 15, 2026 · RJB Contracting

The checkout area is the last stop in a customer's visit, but it leaves one of the strongest impressions.

Long lines, unclear entrances, tight circulation, poorly positioned equipment, and limited space for carts create friction at the exact moment a shopper is ready to buy. A well-planned checkout area does the opposite. It makes the process feel faster, easier to read, and better connected to the rest of the store.

Retailers are adding self-checkout, assisted checkout, mobile point-of-sale, and hybrid front-end formats. The physical layout has to keep up with the technology. That takes more than installing new equipment. It takes accurate site information, careful sequencing, and a clear understanding of how customers, associates, fixtures, utilities, and merchandise move through the space.

For retailers planning a store remodel or national retail rollout, the right construction strategy turns checkout changes into a better customer experience.

Self-Checkout Is a Store-Flow Decision

Self-checkout gets discussed as a technology upgrade. Its impact reaches well past the machines.

A new checkout configuration affects:

  • How customers approach and enter the queue
  • Space available for carts, baskets, and merchandise
  • Sightlines for customers and associates
  • Placement of impulse-purchase displays
  • Power, data, lighting, and security requirements
  • Accessibility and clear-floor-space requirements
  • Loss-prevention visibility and exit control
  • The balance between self-service and staffed lanes

Research from ECR Retail Loss looks at the relationship between self-checkout design, customer participation, scan accuracy, and the overall shopping experience. The takeaway for project teams is practical: layout decisions either support the checkout system or work against it.

Customers should understand where to go without stopping to study the space. Associates need visibility across the area and room to help. The queue should feel organized without blocking adjacent departments, entrances, exits, or accessible routes.

That is why checkout planning starts with customer flow, not an equipment count.

Understanding the Existing Store

Before a retailer can build a repeatable checkout solution, the project team needs reliable information about current conditions.

Stores built from the same prototype drift apart over time. Previous remodels, fixture changes, localized repairs, different utility locations, and site-specific operating needs create real differences from one location to the next.

A thorough site survey program documents details like:

  • Existing checkout and queue configurations
  • Fixture dimensions and clearances
  • Electrical and data locations
  • Floor, wall, and ceiling conditions
  • Entrances, exits, and accessible routes
  • Adjacent merchandising and customer-service areas
  • Cameras, sensors, and other security infrastructure
  • Conditions that affect demolition or installation

RJB Contracting completed a survey program across more than 700 Sheetz locations in three months, using 360-degree site capture for consistent documentation across the portfolio. The information went directly to the client's servers, giving the project team a standardized view of existing conditions across a large, geographically dispersed program.

On multi-site work, that level of documentation helps owners compare locations, refine prototypes, identify exceptions, and make better decisions before crews mobilize.

Turning Survey Data Into a Rollout Plan

Collecting information is the first step. The value comes from turning it into a workable construction plan.

Once existing conditions are understood, retailers can decide what stays consistent across every location and what needs site-specific adjustment. A strong rollout strategy defines the standard scope, approved alternates, responsibilities, material logistics, construction sequence, communication process, and closeout requirements.

For checkout-area renovations, planning covers:

  • Removing or relocating existing registers and fixtures
  • Installing self-checkout units and queue-line components
  • Modifying electrical, data, or low-voltage infrastructure
  • Coordinating new signage, lighting, and merchandising
  • Repairing flooring, ceilings, and finishes after removals
  • Maintaining customer access during phased work
  • Testing equipment and completing turnover before reopening

Standardization creates consistency. It should not replace field judgment. A column in the wrong place, an undocumented floor condition, or a utility conflict can change the planned layout. Clear escalation and approval processes let the field team resolve those conditions without losing control of the program.

Rethinking the Front End at Kohl's

RJB's work on the Kohl's Queue Line Program shows how coordinated construction supports a broader front-end strategy.

In 2025, RJB completed queue-line improvements in 150 stores within three months. The work included self-checkout integration, refreshed queue-line elements, Pepsi fixture updates, and related electrical coordination.

The goal was not to replace equipment. The program reworked the checkout flow to create a more organized path through the front end and make better use of the surrounding space.

RJB continued the program in 2026, completing 20 additional stores across eight states in one month.

Programs at that scale depend on repeatable processes, clear communication, dependable trade coordination, and the ability to work inside active retail environments. Each location has to reflect the intended design while still accounting for real site conditions.

Construction Inside an Active Store

Front-end renovations happen in one of the busiest and most visible areas of a store. That makes phasing and communication critical.

The project team often has to keep part of the checkout area open while other lanes are under construction. Work may run overnight or during slower periods, with temporary routes and barriers adjusted as the project moves.

Common considerations include:

  • Keeping entrances, exits, and accessible paths clear
  • Separating customers from active construction
  • Coordinating noise, dust, and shutdowns around store hours
  • Preserving enough checkout capacity to support operations
  • Communicating daily impacts to store leadership
  • Reopening completed areas safely and on time

The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design include requirements for checkout aisles, sales and service counters, queues, and waiting lines. Applicable accessibility requirements belong in design and construction planning alongside local codes and the retailer's own standards.

In an occupied store, the construction plan has to work for the finished space and for the customers using the building during the renovation.

Balancing Speed, Choice, and Support

No single checkout format works for every retailer or every customer.

Some shoppers want the speed and control of self-checkout. Others prefer a staffed lane, especially with a large cart or when they need help. Research summarized by Drexel University's LeBow College of Business suggests the checkout method, and the effort it asks of the customer, shapes how people perceive the store.

For many retailers the answer is a flexible front end with more than one way to complete a purchase. Construction supports that flexibility by making sure the space can hold the chosen mix of technology, staffed service, customer assistance, and future adjustments.

The strongest layouts account for:

  • Customer choice and ease of use
  • Associate visibility and access
  • Queue capacity during peak periods
  • Transaction size and cart movement
  • Security and loss-prevention needs
  • Accessibility
  • Room for future reconfiguration

Technology will keep changing. A well-coordinated physical environment gives retailers room to adapt with it.

The General Contractor's Role

Checkout renovations pull together equipment vendors, fixture installers, electricians, low-voltage contractors, flooring crews, signage providers, store operations, security teams, and others.

The commercial general contractor connects those moving parts to the overall project schedule. That means validating field conditions, coordinating affected trades, planning shutdowns, managing site controls, tracking materials, communicating operational impacts, documenting changing conditions, and making sure each location is ready for equipment testing and turnover.

On a multi-site rollout, consistency is essential. So is responsiveness. The construction team needs a repeatable process that scales across the program without overlooking the conditions that make each store different.
