Small Scope, Big Coordination: Moving a Retail Checkout

October 29, 2026 · RJB Contracting

Moving a checkout counter may look like a simple change on a floor plan. Relocate the fixtures, connect the registers, and reopen the area.

In practice, that move brings together several connected scopes. Electrical service needs to reach the new location. Data connections need to support the equipment. Flooring may require repairs where the old counters stood. Queue fixtures must fit the approved layout, and customers still need a clear path through the store.

The footprint may be small, but the coordination reaches well beyond it.

RJB’s work on the Kohl’s Queue Line Program offers a practical example of that coordination, with self-checkout integration and electrical work for Pepsi refresh stations completed around store operations.

Start With What the New Layout Requires

Before crews move a fixture, the project team needs to understand how the proposed checkout will function.

The approved plan should establish where equipment belongs, how customers approach it, and how associates access the area. Construction planning then connects those requirements to existing field conditions.

Early coordination should address:

  • Register, counter, and queue fixture locations.
  • Electrical and data connection points.
  • Equipment dimensions and installation requirements.
  • Customer routes into and out of the checkout area.
  • Associate workspaces and service access.
  • Adjacent merchandising, signage, and equipment.
  • Areas that must remain operational during construction.

A preconstruction site survey helps identify conditions that could affect the installation before materials arrive or crews mobilize.

Electrical Work Depends on the Final Equipment Locations

A register relocation can require changes to the infrastructure serving it.

Existing power may be in the wrong location for the new layout. New equipment may have different connection requirements. Nearby coolers or other powered fixtures may need additional electrical work.

The electrical scope should clarify:

  • Which existing connections remain in use.
  • Which connections need to be relocated or removed.
  • Where new circuits or receptacles are required.
  • How wiring will reach the equipment.
  • Whether shutdowns are necessary.
  • How electrical work fits into the installation sequence.

Equipment placement and electrical planning need to agree before installation. A late fixture adjustment can affect a completed connection or require additional work.

Data and Technology Need Their Own Coordination

Power is only one part of getting a checkout ready for use.

Registers and self-checkout equipment also depend on the retailer’s technology systems. Depending on the scope, work may involve data cabling, network connections, payment equipment, or coordination with security systems.

The project team should establish:

  • Who provides and installs the cabling.
  • Who connects and configures the checkout equipment.
  • What must be completed before the retailer’s technology team arrives.
  • When equipment testing will occur.
  • Who confirms the checkout is ready to serve customers.

These responsibilities may sit with different contractors and owner vendors. Clearly defining the handoffs helps avoid an installation that looks complete but cannot yet operate.

Removing Fixtures Can Expose Flooring Repairs

The flooring beneath an existing counter may look different from the surrounding sales floor.

Previous installations can leave anchor holes, penetrations, adhesive residue, unfinished areas, or mismatched materials. Moving the fixtures exposes those conditions.

Before pricing and scheduling the work, teams should consider:

  • What flooring is visible beneath existing equipment.
  • Whether matching replacement material is available.
  • How abandoned penetrations will be addressed.
  • Whether repairs require drying or curing time.
  • How repaired areas will be protected before equipment installation.

For example, a floor repair that needs time to cure must be coordinated with fixture placement. Installing equipment too early can interfere with the repair or create rework.

Queue Fixtures Shape How the Space Works

Counters, barriers, merchandise fixtures, and refresh stations all compete for space at the front of the store.

Their placement affects how customers enter the queue, approach an available register, and leave after completing a purchase. It also affects associate access and the visibility of nearby signs.

Before installation, confirm:

  • The latest approved fixture layout.
  • Dimensions and assembly requirements.
  • Anchoring requirements.
  • Clearances around equipment and service points.
  • Delivery timing and storage arrangements.
  • Responsibility for installation and final placement.

A cooler or merchandising fixture added near the checkout can influence both utility planning and customer movement. Reviewing the front end as one connected area helps the team identify those relationships.

Keep Checkout Available During the Work

In an operating store, checkout construction needs a plan for maintaining service.

That may involve completing work in phases, keeping selected registers available, or scheduling disruptive activities outside normal shopping hours. The approach depends on the retailer’s requirements and the work being performed.

Coordination with store operations should cover:

  • Which checkout areas remain available.
  • How customers will be directed around the work.
  • When equipment can be disconnected.
  • Where materials and tools can be staged.
  • How work areas will be separated from shoppers.
  • What must be cleaned, secured, or restored before opening.
  • Who approves the transition to the updated checkout.

Daily communication matters. Store associates need to know what is changing, when it will happen, and how it affects their work.

The Kohl’s Queue Line Program in Practice

RJB’s Kohl’s Queue Line Program involved self-checkout register installations and electrical work for new Pepsi refresh stations.

The work connected new equipment to existing store infrastructure while crews coordinated around store hours to maintain checkout operations. Repeating the scope across multiple locations required consistent installation planning and attention to each store’s conditions.

The program illustrates an important construction principle: front-end upgrades depend on the relationship between equipment, infrastructure, and operations.

The broader electrical, data, flooring, and fixture considerations discussed in this article should be evaluated for each checkout project. The required scope will vary by location and retailer.

Repeating the Scope Requires a Clear Process

For a single store, a missed detail can disrupt an installation. Across a multi-site rollout, the same gap can affect several locations.

A repeatable process should establish:

  • A standard scope and approved layout.
  • Location-specific conditions and exceptions.
  • Equipment and material delivery requirements.
  • Contractor and vendor responsibilities.
  • Work windows and installation sequences.
  • A process for requesting clarification or approval.
  • Completion documentation and turnover requirements.

Consistency helps teams prepare for the work. Clear escalation procedures help them respond when a store differs from the standard plan.

Completion Means Ready for Use

A checkout area should be evaluated as an operating space before it is turned over.

The construction team, store representatives, and relevant vendors should confirm that their assigned work is complete and that remaining issues have an owner.

The final review should address:

  • Fixtures installed in the approved locations.
  • Electrical work completed and required testing documented.
  • Data and technology readiness confirmed by the responsible team.
  • Flooring repairs and surrounding finishes completed.
  • Customer and associate routes reviewed against the approved plan.
  • Debris, tools, and temporary controls removed as appropriate.
  • Outstanding items documented and assigned.

A finished counter is one part of the result. A checkout ready to support store operations is the goal.

Planning a Checkout Upgrade

Retail checkout relocations bring several trades and stakeholders into a concentrated work area. Understanding those connections early helps teams build a workable scope, sequence the installation, and coordinate turnover.
