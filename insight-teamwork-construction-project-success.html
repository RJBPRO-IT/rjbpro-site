How RJB's Culture of Collaboration Supports Project Success

September 20, 2026 · RJB Contracting

Commercial construction moves through a series of handoffs. Estimating, preconstruction, project management, field supervision, subcontractors, suppliers, the client.

Each person owns a different part of the work. The project only succeeds when those parts stay connected.

That makes teamwork more than a workplace value. It is a project-control system. When information moves quickly, responsibilities are clear, and people follow through, teams handle changing conditions before they turn into schedule, cost, or quality problems.

At RJB Contracting, collaboration shapes how we approach commercial construction, renovations, and rollout programs. It connects the people planning the work with the people building it, and gives clients a more coordinated project.

Construction Depends on Connected Decisions

Even a well-planned project needs decisions in the field.

An unexpected existing condition changes a trade's scope. A material delay forces resequencing. A change in site access alters deliveries, staffing, or operating hours. Each issue reaches past the person who found it.

Strong teamwork creates a reliable path for moving that information to the people who can evaluate the impact and act. The goal is not to put everyone in every conversation. It is to get the right information to the right person at the right time.

That discipline lets the team make informed decisions while keeping downstream work in view.

What Collaboration Looks Like in Practice

Collaboration is worth most when it shows up in everyday behavior. On a construction project, that means:

  • Asking questions before assumptions turn into rework
  • Sharing changing conditions with everyone whose work is affected
  • Speaking directly about risks, delays, and missed commitments
  • Assigning clear ownership and next steps
  • Following through and confirming an issue is closed
  • Respecting the experience of field, office, trade, and client teams

These sound simple. They take consistency. A project can have capable people and still struggle when information stays isolated or accountability is unclear.

The Field and Office Need the Same Picture

Field and office teams see a project from different angles. Both matter.

Superintendents know current site conditions, sequencing, access, and daily trade coordination. Project managers connect that to contracts, procurement, documentation, client communication, and the overall schedule. Estimating, purchasing, accounting, safety, and leadership add the support needed to resolve an issue.

The strongest decisions reflect both perspectives without slowing the work down.

Consistent reports, photographs, schedule updates, requests for information, and direct conversations build a shared understanding of where the project stands. When something changes, a clear escalation path tells the team who decides, what information they need, and how the response affects the work that follows.

Collaboration Protects the Schedule

A construction schedule is a network of connected activities. Change one task and several others move.

A recent fire-pump project near RJB's headquarters involved underground boring and a temporary roadway shutdown. The work took coordination among fire-protection professionals, utility teams, traffic control, facility operations, inspections, and the construction schedule.

The technical work mattered. So did the handoffs. Site access had to stay controlled. The shutdown had to happen at the right time. Affected parties needed accurate updates. Following work could not start until the required conditions were met.

The same principle applies to renovations and multi-location programs. The earlier a team spots a constraint and communicates its impact, the more options it has to protect the schedule.

The Collaborative Way at RJB

RJB's approach is reinforced through The Collaborative Way, a framework developed by Jason and Lloyd Fickett to help organizations build stronger working relationships and more accountable teams.

RJB employees train on behaviors like listening carefully, speaking directly, honoring commitments, showing appreciation, and supporting one another.

That focus also supports how people hold up under pressure. A Gallup study of nearly 7,500 full-time employees found 23% felt burned out at work very often or always, and another 44% felt burned out sometimes. Gallup also found employees who strongly felt supported by their manager were about 70% less likely to experience burnout regularly. Collaboration alone does not remove workplace pressure. Clear responsibilities, frequent communication, and dependable support address some of what causes it.

In construction, those behaviors have practical consequences. Listening surfaces a concern before work starts. Speaking directly keeps uncertainty from lingering. Honoring commitments builds confidence between teams. Appreciation recognizes the effort it takes to solve hard problems together.

The framework does not promise teams will always agree or that projects will never hit pressure. It gives everyone a shared way to handle those moments.

What Clients Experience

Clients do not see every internal conversation. They see the results.

They get clearer information about current conditions and next steps. Decisions reach the field faster. Schedule impacts come earlier. Questions get answered by a coordinated team instead of bouncing between disconnected parties.

Unexpected conditions still happen, especially in existing buildings and active commercial environments. The difference is how the team responds: with transparency, ownership, and a plan that accounts for the whole project.

Collaboration Has to Scale

Teamwork matters more when construction programs run across multiple locations.

Each site has different conditions, operating requirements, permitting processes, and local trade partners. Consistent reporting, defined responsibilities, and repeatable communication give owners visibility while letting project teams respond to what is actually in front of them.

That balance, standardized process supported by local judgment, is how a contractor delivers a consistent client experience without treating every site as identical.
