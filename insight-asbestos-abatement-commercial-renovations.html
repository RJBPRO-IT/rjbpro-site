Biohazards on Site: Managing Asbestos During Commercial Renovations

July 20, 2026 · RJB Contracting

In commercial construction, some of the biggest project risks are the ones you can't see.

Asbestos-containing materials (ACM) still turn up in existing retail, grocery, office, warehouse, and other commercial buildings. They surface when renovation or demolition work starts opening walls, removing flooring, accessing mechanical systems, or disturbing older building materials.

Finding suspected asbestos doesn't mean a project stops. It means the next steps have to be deliberate.

For owners and facility teams planning a commercial renovation, remodel, or tenant improvement, understanding how asbestos gets identified and managed protects occupants and construction teams while limiting surprises to the schedule.

Where Asbestos Can Appear During a Commercial Renovation

Asbestos went into many building products for its heat resistance, strength, and insulating properties. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency lists building materials where it may be found, including:

  • Floor tile and associated mastics
  • Pipe and mechanical insulation
  • Ceiling tile and acoustical materials
  • Spray-applied fireproofing
  • Roofing and siding materials
  • Cementitious panels and products
  • Plaster and surfacing materials
  • Certain insulation and building components

The presence of asbestos-containing material does not automatically create an exposure. The concern rises when construction activities cut, drill, sand, demolish, remove, or otherwise disturb the material and release fibers.

That makes preconstruction investigation critical on renovation projects, where existing conditions aren't always documented.

Before Demolition Starts: Know What's Behind the Walls

A strong commercial renovation strategy starts before demolition.

For commercial and industrial buildings subject to federal asbestos regulations, the EPA requires a thorough inspection of the affected facility, or the affected portion of it, before applicable demolition or renovation work begins. The Asbestos NESHAP requirements also set notification and work-practice rules for certain projects.

Building owners and project teams should review existing asbestos surveys, inspection records, drawings, and other available documentation when planning work. When suspect materials show up, qualified asbestos professionals determine whether sampling, testing, further investigation, or abatement is required.

This early coordination matters. Discovering asbestos after demolition begins creates safety concerns, schedule disruptions, added cost, restricted work areas, and coordination problems.

Identifying potential hazardous materials during preconstruction lets the project team build the response into the project instead of reacting to it in the field.

What Happens When Asbestos Is Found?

The right response depends on the material, its condition, the planned construction activity, applicable regulations, and how much may be disturbed.

Depending on those factors, asbestos-containing material may be managed in place, encapsulated, enclosed, repaired, or removed through a controlled abatement process.

When abatement is necessary, the work can involve:

  • Isolating the affected work area
  • Establishing appropriate containment
  • Using negative-pressure systems where required
  • Using HEPA filtration and vacuums
  • Applying approved wet methods to limit airborne fibers
  • Following worker protection and decontamination procedures
  • Properly packaging and transporting asbestos-containing waste
  • Completing required inspections or air monitoring before reoccupancy

Requirements vary by project and jurisdiction. Federal rules from the EPA and OSHA's asbestos standards may apply alongside state and local regulations.

Asbestos Abatement in an Occupied Facility

Managing hazardous materials gets harder when construction happens inside an active commercial environment.

Retail stores, grocery stores, offices, distribution facilities, and other operating locations often need to stay partially or fully functional while renovations run.

In those environments, the challenge isn't finishing the abatement. It's coordinating the abatement with the larger construction schedule.

That means sequencing demolition around abatement activities, maintaining separation between construction and occupied spaces, coordinating shutdowns or restricted areas, communicating with facility teams, and determining when following trades can safely enter the affected area.

For nationwide retailers and facility operators, that coordination matters even more when renovations run across multiple locations with different site conditions and jurisdictional requirements.

The General Contractor's Role

Asbestos removal is performed by qualified and licensed professionals as the jurisdiction requires. But hazardous-material work takes coordination well beyond the abatement contractor.

The commercial general contractor integrates abatement into the overall construction process. That means coordinating affected trades, adjusting sequencing, maintaining site controls, communicating schedule impacts, documenting changing conditions, and making sure construction doesn't move into an affected area before it has been properly addressed.

On a renovation project, asbestos rarely exists in isolation.

Removing flooring affects the finish schedule. Opening a wall affects electrical or plumbing work. Abatement above a ceiling changes when HVAC, fire protection, or lighting work can start.

Each decision affects the work that follows.

Planning for the Unexpected

Renovations in existing buildings always carry uncertainty. Drawings may be incomplete. Previous renovations may not be documented. Conditions behind walls, ceilings, and floors may differ from what the team expected.

The goal isn't to pretend those unknowns don't exist. It's to have a process for handling them.

At RJB Contracting, our teams approach commercial renovations with an emphasis on planning, communication, safety, and coordination. When unforeseen conditions or hazardous materials come up, we work with the owner, environmental professionals, qualified abatement contractors, and project stakeholders to determine the path forward and build it into the construction schedule.

For clients managing renovations across multiple locations, that ability to adapt matters even more.

The best construction projects aren't the ones where nothing unexpected happens. They're the ones where the unexpected is managed well.
